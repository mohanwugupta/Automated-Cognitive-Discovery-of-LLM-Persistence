\begin{abstract}
Cognitive models describe how decisions depend on computational quantities, offering mechanistic interpretability targets beyond behavioral labels. We study a discovery workflow that fits behavioral theories, freezes their computational counterfactuals, searches for neural subspaces that reproduce those transformations, and tests the resulting interventions on held-out conditions. In a seven-task persistence battery with Qwen3.5-4B, history- and evidence-dependent models predict continuing-versus-disengaging decisions with task-specific calibration. Their counterfactuals guide distributed alignment search to low-dimensional controllers with held-out recovery of 0.930--0.968 and task-holdout recovery of 0.673--0.878. A five-seed follow-up of the primary controller retains strong original-contrast recovery. The evidence nevertheless separates discovery success from mechanistic identification: integrated persistence evidence is a candidate interpretation, and fresh contrasts expose poor quantitative generalization of the cognitive targets. A secondary diagnostic nevertheless finds that the primary controller closely reproduces natural history-change effects across fresh contexts. A frozen test on unrestricted generation also fails to establish a reliable dose effect on EOS stopping hazard. This case study demonstrates how cognitive models can constrain causal search and make its limits testable: successful intervention recovery on selected structured contrasts does not establish a uniquely identified or universal persistence mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
As language models increasingly act toward assigned goals, understanding when they continue and when they stop becomes a question about the computations that organize their behavior. Research on goal-directedness distinguishes the use of a model's capabilities toward an objective from raw task performance \citep{everitt2025goals,arghal2026goals}. Human work on voluntary waiting, foraging, and quitting offers candidate accounts of persistence in terms of expected benefits, accumulated evidence, and the value of alternative actions \citep{mcguire2015persistence,constantino2015foraging,sukhov2025quitting}. These accounts can motivate controlled experiments on language models without assuming that apparently similar behavior has the same underlying mechanism \citep{binz2023psychology,shanahan2023roleplay}.

The mechanistic question is harder than asking whether a relevant variable is decodable. A hidden state can predict future reward without that particular representation controlling the decision to continue. Conversely, a direction trained on the decision logit may steer behavior while revealing little about the computation that produces it. The original \emph{Sisyphus in the Loop} study illustrates this distinction: in a sequential bandit with a STOP action, future return was decodable, whereas the fitted return direction showed little causal effect and a late persistence-aligned direction supplied a positive control \citep{gupta2026sisyphus}. That result motivates a discovery problem: how can we use explicit behavioral theories to find neural transformations relevant to persistence?

Our approach uses computational-cognitive models as intervention specifications. A theory predicts not just the response associated with a history, but how that response should change if one computational quantity is replaced while other inputs remain fixed. We freeze those counterfactual predictions and fit a low-dimensional neural intervention to reproduce them. The resulting workflow is \emph{behavioral theory $\rightarrow$ computational counterfactual $\rightarrow$ causal subspace search $\rightarrow$ held-out intervention test}. Distributed alignment search supplies the optimization method \citep{geiger2024alignments,wu2023scale}; cognitive modeling supplies structured targets and explicit alternatives whose consequences can be tested.

Persistence provides a demanding case study because continuing can reflect current incentives, recent actions and outcomes, inferred context, or combinations of these factors. Across seven structured tasks, the behavioral evidence supports a shared history- and evidence-dependent form with task-specific calibration. Frozen models then guide the discovery of neural controllers that recover predicted effects on held-out examples and task families excluded from neural fitting. This is the main positive result: the computational models are useful for specifying and finding causal transformations even though their variables need not map one-to-one onto isolated neural features.

The same framework makes stronger interpretations falsifiable. Follow-up analyses motivate an integrated persistence-evidence state $E$, but do not establish its specificity over matched random subspaces. New signed-history contrasts show that strong original-contrast recovery does not ensure calibrated recovery even throughout the structured task family. A broader context test attributes part of this boundary to the behavioral targets themselves, while a secondary diagnostic retains strong recovery of natural history-change effects. Finally, without OOD selection or retuning, the chosen controller fails a test of voluntary continuation in unrestricted generation. The contribution is therefore a demonstrated strategy for constraining mechanistic discovery and measuring its scope, rather than the identification of a universal ``keep going'' variable. The evidence supports held-out causal control on selected structured contrasts; the identities and broader generality of the underlying mechanisms remain open.

\section{Cognitive models as targets for mechanistic discovery}\label{sec:framework}
Let $x$ denote a rendered task condition, including its prescribed history, current evidence, and response mapping. We measure the semantic choice logit
\begin{equation}
g(x)=\ell_{\mathrm{continue}}(x)-\ell_{\mathrm{disengage}}(x),
\end{equation}
using verified single-token action labels. The associated binary probability is conditional on those two actions. It is distinct from their total probability mass in the full vocabulary; the collection pipeline records that mass and checks response validity.

\paragraph{From a theory to a transformation.}
A behavioral model $f$ assigns a computational prediction to each condition. Writing the proposed variable as $z$ and the other model inputs as $c$, the counterfactual target is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i^f=f(c_b,z_s)-f(c_b,z_b).
\label{eq:counterfactual}
\end{equation}
For example, changing past outcomes while holding current incentives and action history fixed yields a predicted change in the semantic choice logit. This specifies both a direction and a magnitude, rather than only a label for decoding. For a source/base pair $(s,b)$ and a proposed variable $z$, its interchange counterfactual specifies an effect $\Delta_i^f$ obtained by substituting the source value of $z$ into the base computation. These targets are computed before neural optimization. Model alternatives remain in the frozen bank when behavior does not discriminate them.

\paragraph{From a transformation to a neural intervention.}
For a residual state $h_b$ and an orthonormal basis $U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$, the neural interchange is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde h_b=h_b+UU^\top(h_s-h_b).
\end{equation}
The intervention replaces only the source-aligned coordinates at the final prompt token. The remaining residual coordinates and the rest of the prompt are preserved. The induced change in semantic logit, $\Delta_i^N$, is compared with $\Delta_i^f$. This tests an input-output transformation; it does not, by itself, show that the same coordinates naturally carry the proposed variable or are necessary for the behavior. Subspace patching can produce an intended causal effect through pathways that do not justify the intended interpretation \citep{makelov2024illusion}.

\paragraph{Held-out tests and interpretation.}
We distinguish five empirical questions. First, does a cognitive model predict held-out behavior? Second, does a neural intervention reproduce its quantitative counterfactuals? Third, does recovery transfer to excluded task families? Fourth, is the intervention specific to the proposed computation relative to alternative targets and matched controls? Fifth, does the frozen state generalize beyond the discovery task family? Each question imposes additional requirements; a positive answer to an earlier one does not settle the later ones.

\section{Behavioral discovery across persistence tasks}\label{sec:behavior}
The task compiler varies an explicit ontology of history, current state, and choice conditions. Counterbalanced X/Y mappings separate semantic continuing from a particular output token. Histories are prescribed evidence, not trajectories generated by the model's previous choices. We therefore interpret the measurements as responses to experimental histories, rather than as naturally evolving long-horizon agency.

\paragraph{Computational alternatives.}
The model bank contrasts current-state accounts, action perseveration, outcome integration, joint action/outcome history, and context-sensitive history, alongside flexible predictors. In the history accounts, recent actions and outcomes enter separately through the latest observation and a recency-weighted trace. The fixed kernel weights lag $k$ by $0.7^k$; semantic actions code continuing as $+1$ and disengaging as $-1$. Dual history combines these action and outcome features with current-state predictors, including continuation and outside-option values, cost, progress, success evidence, and uncertainty. The context account instead uses relevant history and interactions with goal continuity and environmental stability. These are explicit competing operationalizations, not assumed neural variables.

The initial design uses pair/episode-safe discovery, interpolation, and structural partitions. The implemented behavioral bank includes grounded cognitive models and flexible comparison models. Subsequent rounds examine parameter sharing, task transfer, residual structure, and targeted discrimination between surviving accounts. The strongest hierarchical interpolation result is dual history with task-structured calibration, reported at task-macro $R^2=0.738$. This supports shared computational form with heterogeneous parameters, not one invariant set of coefficients across tasks.

The untouched final behavioral validation reports pooled $R^2=0.862$, task-macro $R^2=0.763$, RMSE $0.491$, and calibration slope $0.824$. The pooled and task-macro statistics answer different questions and should not be substituted for each other. Dual-history and latent-context accounts remain unresolved by the reported behavioral comparisons. Model-recovery diagnostics and alternative accounts are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}. Retaining multiple viable models allows counterfactual search to proceed without pretending that behavioral prediction has uniquely identified the computation.

\section{Computationally guided causal discovery}\label{sec:causal}
\subsection{Frozen targets and selection}
The causal workflow separates behavioral-model freezing, counterfactual-pair construction, layer localization, DAS fitting, and evaluation. The neural discovery tasks are bandit, debugging, foraging, and solvability; effort, information sampling, and waiting form the task holdout. The selected neural subsets preserve separate train, validation, pair-test, and task-holdout roles (Appendix~\ref{app:selection}).

Whole-state patching provides candidate layers using validation data. The configured DAS search examines ranks $1,2,4,8$, with three epochs, learning rate $0.03$, and an activation-change penalty of $10^{-4}$. Layer/rank selection uses validation outcomes and the configured preference for smaller ranks within an MSE tolerance. Reported test and task-holdout outcomes do not enter the fitting loop.

Three shared alignments were retained. We report all three to avoid presenting the descriptive test maximum as if it were an independently selected winner. The later abstraction and OOD studies freeze the layer-28/rank-2 dual-history alignment. Those follow-ups are additional tests of a chosen controller, not independent replications of its discovery.

\subsection{Evaluation metric and results}
We score the learned transformation with global counterfactual recovery,
\begin{equation}
\cfr=1-\frac{\sum_i(\Delta_i^N-\Delta_i^f)^2}{\sum_i(0-\Delta_i^f)^2}.
\end{equation}
Here zero denotes no neural intervention effect. A score of one is exact recovery, zero matches the no-effect baseline, and negative scores are worse. The metric is undefined when the denominator vanishes. Unlike the mean of per-example relative recovery, this aggregate avoids dividing separately by small target effects. Nevertheless, high recovery can coexist with limited within-task discrimination, so we also examine effect correlation, calibration, controls, and target variation.

\subsection{Optimization stability and fixed-controller comparisons}
A fresh Qwen run fixes the primary alignment at layer 28/rank 2 and trains five initialization/data-order seeds on the original 24 training pairs. Every seed is retained. Original pair-test recovery is $0.954\pm0.033$ (sample SD; range 0.900--0.983), and task-holdout recovery is $0.863\pm0.051$. These results support local optimization stability, while varying subspace geometry cautions against treating the fitted coordinates as unique.

At the same layer and rank, direct behavior-target DAS attains recovery 0.844/0.759 on original pair-test/task-holdout contrasts. Training-only history and persistence ridge directions, mean differences, and PCA attain 0.168--0.252/0.178--0.229 under intervention norms matched to the frozen cognitive controller. Scalar directions receive an explicitly specified orthogonal train-PCA coordinate to complete rank 2. The comparison shows that the cognitive-target controller is effective under this fixed evaluation setup; it does not establish superior full-search sample or compute efficiency. Table~\ref{tab:runpod} and Appendix~\ref{app:followup} report the complete comparison, every seed's role, and the new diagnostic where this advantage does not persist.

\section{Interpreting the discovered state}\label{sec:interpretation}
A cognitive variable can be useful for discovering a neural transformation without identifying the natural content of the resulting subspace. A downstream state may integrate several upstream variables, allowing more than one computational model to predict its intervention effects. We therefore test the interpretation of the chosen controller after freezing it, rather than naming its coordinates from the training objective alone.

\paragraph{Integrated persistence evidence as a candidate.}
The abstraction study contrasts raw outcome history $O$, context-sensitive history $O^*$, integrated history contribution $H$, and total persistence evidence $E$. Here $E$ is operationalized as the full pre-decision prediction of the frozen reference behavioral model, rather than an independently established neural quantity. Across six dissociation families, the descriptive analyses favor $E$: natural-state geometry reaches held-out $R^2=0.700$, with intervention recovery 0.707 and effect correlation 0.882. This motivates the downstream integrated-evidence account in the proposed scientific arc. However, the matched-random comparison fails ($p=0.667$), so the evidence does not establish that the subspace specifically represents $E$. The design and competing abstractions appear in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}.

\paragraph{New counterfactuals reveal a calibration boundary.}
We next test four signed history changes per task and response mapping, frozen before neural evaluation. The 56 fresh pairs preserve action history and current factors within each source/base pair and provide distinct target magnitudes and both signs. They are evaluation-only. Background coverage is narrow and differs across response mappings in four tasks, limiting response-label invariance claims.

The frozen controller's effects track the new targets (correlations 0.935 on familiar tasks and 0.838 on held-out tasks), yet global recovery falls to 0.080 and $-0.426$. Target derangements genuinely change all target values and score below the true assignment, but the controller does not establish fresh matched-random specificity ($p=0.095$ and $0.952$). Some conventional baselines attain higher fresh recovery. Thus correspondence in direction is insufficient for calibrated computational equivalence. This result narrows the positive cross-task claim to the evaluated contrast distribution; it cannot be relegated to an OOD-only caveat. Full diagnostics, including the uninformative original shuffle and all 3,720 new intervention evaluations, appear in Appendix~\ref{app:followup}.

\paragraph{Separating target calibration from natural-effect recovery.}
A broader frozen test uses four new current-state backgrounds per task, four signed history patterns of lengths three and five, and identical semantic states across response mappings. All three retained controllers are evaluated without refitting. The primary controller attains cognitive-target recovery 0.268 on familiar tasks and 0.026 on held-out tasks. A secondary diagnostic, specified during this run, measures the model's natural source-minus-base effects on these same controlled contrasts. Against those effects, primary-controller normalized recovery is 0.894/0.871, with correlations 0.953/0.924. The cognitive targets themselves have recovery $-0.168/-0.393$ against natural effects. Thus weak cognitive-target calibration cannot be attributed entirely to the neural intervention: an imperfect behavioral model can guide discovery of a controller that tracks natural changes more closely than its original quantitative targets. This diagnostic does not replace the primary endpoint, establish cognitive-variable identity, or measure the fraction of a mechanism causally mediated. Appendix~\ref{app:freshcontexts} reports all controllers and controls.

\section{A frozen boundary test: unrestricted generation}\label{sec:ood}
The OOD study asks whether the structured-task state controls voluntary continuation of free generation. The alignment is hash-frozen; a signed E-oriented axis is derived from 2,368 pre-OOD structured activation rows. The layer, rank, doses, prompts, decoding policy, and statistical plan are frozen before generation. The experiment intervenes on the newly processed final-token residual and keeps canonical EOS tokens available. There is no application-level output-token cap; context exhaustion, infrastructure timeout, and resource exhaustion are separately classified censoring events.

The primary battery contains six prompts, 50 matched seeds, and five doses, yielding 1,500 generations. All primary generations terminate with EOS; none is context-censored. In the current committed gates, the frozen protocol and both cache checks pass.

The prompt-stratified Cox estimate for dose is $\beta=-0.0138$, with 95\% interval $[-0.0501,0.0225]$. The dose/RMST Spearman statistic is $0.100$, and the random-specificity test reports $p=0.257$. Direction, monotonicity, and random-specificity gates fail. Some secondary outcomes have the hypothesized sign, but they do not satisfy the full primary criterion. The justified conclusion is failure to establish the registered OOD generalization claim, rather than proof that the controller has exactly zero influence on stopping or that no domain-general persistence mechanism exists anywhere in the model.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Behavioral models of goal pursuit.}
Goal-directedness measures whether a model uses its capabilities toward an assigned objective, which can differ from task performance \citep{everitt2025goals}. Behavioral and representational evaluations also probe how navigation agents encode goals and plans \citep{arghal2026goals}. Cognitive psychology provides controlled experiments for characterizing language-model behavior \citep{binz2023psychology,aher2023simulate}. Human persistence and foraging models motivate candidate computations, but analogous behavior does not establish a shared mechanism; a prompted role can elicit apparently psychological behavior \citep{shanahan2023roleplay}. We use these literatures to construct falsifiable predictions rather than to infer human motivations.

\paragraph{Decision-making and internal reward information.}
Bandit research identifies greediness and a gap between a model's knowledge and its choices \citep{schmied2025greedy}; relative-value biases provide a computational account of some bandit choices \citep{hayes2024biased}. A STOP option adds the decision to keep participating. Work on sparse reward-related activations investigates internal value information and its intervention effects in reasoning \citep{xu2026reward}; whether a particular reward representation governs stopping is a separate empirical question. Activation directions can control refusal and persona-related behavior \citep{arditi2024refusal,chen2025persona}, supporting intervention as an empirical test while leaving each proposed mechanism to be established. Causal alignment and subspace-patching research supply tools and cautions for linking computational variables to interventions \citep{geiger2024alignments,wu2023scale,makelov2024illusion}.

\section{Discussion}
The central methodological lesson is that cognitive models can constrain neural search through transformations with quantitative behavioral consequences. In this case study, that approach finds low-dimensional interventions that recover held-out effects and transfer across selected task contrasts. The behavioral theory does useful work even when its variables cannot be assigned one-to-one to neural features: it supplies targets, alternative predictions, and tests that a successful controller must face.

This provides more structured evidence than decodability alone. A probe asks whether a quantity can be recovered from a state; the present intervention test asks whether a neural replacement reproduces a specified computational consequence on held-out inputs. Neither steerability nor counterfactual recovery, however, establishes the model's natural causal implementation by itself. Subspace ambiguity, correlated theoretical predictions, necessity tests, and appropriate controls remain consequential \citep{makelov2024illusion}. Our results demonstrate the workflow, not the invention of DAS or a completed proof of its superiority over alternative discovery methods.

The resulting scientific boundary has two levels. Within the structured battery, original-contrast effects are reproducible but frozen cognitive targets are poorly calibrated on fresh histories. Secondary natural-effect diagnostics retain substantial controller fidelity, separating target-model limitations from intervention limitations; the integrated-$E$ identity remains unresolved. Beyond the battery, the frozen intervention fails to establish the predicted change in unrestricted-generation stopping. The latter is a failed generalization test of a particular controller and intervention recipe, not proof that no model-wide persistence computation exists. Together, the tests show why causal control, computational identity, and domain generality must be distinguished.

The main limitations are one model, small selected neural test sets, limited linguistic and fresh-context coverage, and incomplete stability analysis of the full layer/rank search. The new comparisons hold layer and rank fixed; they do not equate discovery budgets. Historical model revisions were unrecorded, so the follow-up uses a pinned checkpoint and recomputed baselines while reporting numerical drift. Better calibrated behavioral targets, broader linguistic coverage, stronger identification controls, and a second-model behavioral fit and mechanistic replication are the next tests of the method's reach. These limitations bound the findings without undoing the positive result: computational-cognitive theories provide an actionable and falsifiable route from behavioral explanation to neural intervention.

\section{Original sequential study: measurement boundaries}
The original report's activation bank contained 512 episodes, partitioned into 358 training, 76 validation, and 78 test episodes. Its main return-decoding comparison used 1,800 test states; an exact-matching analysis retained 1,180 states in 147 strata from 55 episodes. These episode-level partitions differ from the semantic contrast partitions of the expanded task battery.

The original prompt instructed the model to maximize points, specified rewards of $+3$ and losses of $-2$, and offered actions A, B, or C (STOP). Arm probabilities, cumulative score, and round number were not explicitly supplied; earlier conversation turns remained available. Episodes ended at STOP or a 100-decision cap. In the factorial follow-up, only the current decision's STOP payoff and CONTINUE bonus changed, with the underlying conversation history held fixed. All 12 incentive combinations were applied to each of 1,187 held-out states, yielding 14,244 observations.

The report selected a future-return ridge probe at layer 1, a provisional continuation-advantage probe at layer 2, and a persistence positive control at layer 31 using validation data. Advantage estimates used ten forced-action continuations per action and state; because STOP was fixed at zero in the original task, the estimated advantage correlated 0.990 with mean continuation value. This collinearity limits claims of a distinct advantage representation. Likewise, future-return decoding reversed its correlation in negative-value environments, limiting an interpretation as universally calibrated value.

Steering altered the final prompt position at the selected decoder block. The reported calibration used validation states and constrained both decoded shift and activation-standardized RMS before confirmatory evaluation. Twenty coordinatewise sign-randomized directions matched native layer and intervention scale; inference resampled 48 confirmatory episodes. The original experiment's 224,343 steering observations therefore do not constitute independent episodes. Its zero-payoff prompt-framing control also shifted persistence by $-1.444$ logits relative to the unmodified task, which prevents treating the zero/zero factorial cell as an identical baseline. These details are preserved from the original manuscript; the present artifact audit does not independently revalidate that repository's raw outputs.

\section{Probe motivation, alternative theories, and specificity diagnostics}\label{app:diagnostics}
\paragraph{What the original draft established and left open.}
The original report used Qwen3.5-4B with actions A and B yielding stochastic rewards and C terminating the episode. Its persistence measure was $\log(\exp\ell_A+\exp\ell_B)-\ell_C$, whereas the later battery uses a binary semantic contrast. The report gives held-out future-return ridge $R^2=0.240$ and a within-state relative-incentive $R^2=0.784$ from crossing STOP and CONTINUE payoffs on 1,187 states from 48 episodes. Its persistence-direction steering contrast was approximately 1.99 logits, while the fitted return and provisional advantage directions had near-zero effects at the tested scale. These figures are attributed to the original report, not recomputed by the current artifact audit. They motivate the expanded study; the sequential states, behavioral estimands, and intervention calibrations are not pooled with the later structured contrasts. In particular, the null steering results do not exclude nonlinear, distributed, differently located, or dynamically constructed value computations.


\paragraph{Behavioral alternatives and recovery.}
Dual-history and latent-context accounts remain unresolved; the final behavioral validation reports task-macro $R^2=0.763$ and pooled $R^2=0.862$. The Round-2 flexible-teacher report records 30 of 36 passed recovery checks. This motivates retaining model uncertainty rather than reading descriptive rank order as unique theory identification.

\subsection{Matched controls and an uninformative shuffle}
The dual-history controller exceeds 500 matched random subspaces on recovery ($p=0.0020$). The later comparison reports persistence-output recovery $0.485$ and persistence-state recovery $0.264$ on its matched test rows. These comparisons support non-random causal control under their evaluation conditions. They do not constitute a complete equal-data, equal-budget comparison of discovery methods, and should not be described as such.

The same stage marks shuffled-target specificity as failed. Inspection of the stored rows reveals why that outcome is not an informative test for the two rank-2 history controllers. Their target effects are constant within each task up to numerical precision. Although the implementation deranges semantic contrast identifiers within task, it leaves all 24 target values unchanged to a tolerance of $10^{-10}$. Maximum changes are below $1.2\times10^{-15}$. Recovery against those shuffled targets must therefore remain essentially identical.

This is a limitation of the selected contrast set and null design. It neither establishes specificity nor demonstrates its absence. The latent-context shuffle changes 7 of 18 values, but also contains a task with essentially constant targets. This limitation motivated the new signed-history test in Appendix~\ref{app:followup}, whose target variation and permutation changes are verified explicitly. The original and follow-up results remain separate.

Target-selective necessity is also unresolved. In the stable-CFR follow-up, the necessity-job manifest is empty because no candidate passes the preceding specificity gate. Blank necessity scores in this stage are therefore not an empirical estimate of zero necessity. Earlier exploratory necessity artifacts should be described separately from this unexecuted, gated test.

\subsection{Factorial abstraction study}
A separate study freezes the layer-28/rank-2 controller and contrasts raw outcome history $O$, context-sensitive history $O^*$, integrated history $H$, and total evidence $E$. Its design contains 1,400 contrast rows across six dissociation families and 2,400 semantic conditions. The largest absolute target-difference correlation remains $0.849$, so the design improves discrimination without making the abstractions independent.

The descriptive and intervention analyses favor $E$: held-out natural-state geometry reaches $R^2=0.700$, and intervention recovery is $0.707$ with effect correlation $0.882$. The leading interpretation exceeds the stored shuffled-target and persistence/output comparisons. However, it fails the matched-random comparison ($p=0.667$), and the overall abstraction gate is false. These results motivate integrated evidence as a hypothesis but do not uniquely identify an E representation. Positive descriptive fits must be interpreted alongside the failed control that limits their specificity.

\section{Completed Qwen follow-up: full protocol and results}\label{app:followup}
\subsection{Design and fitting}
We ran a prospective follow-up on a RunPod L40S using Qwen3.5-4B checkpoint \texttt{851bf6e...cd0a}; the full immutable SHA and environment are recorded with the artifacts. The primary dual-history controller's layer 28 and rank 2 were fixed. Five initialization/data-order seeds trained on the same 24 original training pairs for three epochs, with learning rate 0.03 and activation-change penalty $10^{-4}$. Every seed is retained. This measures optimization stability conditional on the prior layer/rank selection, not stability of the complete discovery procedure.

The comparison includes direct behavior-target DAS, ridge history and persistence probes, mean differences, PCA, and 20 random subspaces. All conventional fits use original training conditions. Scalar directions are completed to rank 2 with an orthogonal training-PCA direction. Comparison interventions match the frozen controller's per-pair norm; median relative discrepancies from bfloat16 arithmetic remain below 0.006 for every method. These are fixed-layer/rank comparisons, not equal-budget reruns of alternative discovery pipelines.

Before neural evaluation, we constructed four signed history changes for each of seven tasks and two response mappings: one or two early outcomes change sign while action history and current factors remain fixed. The 56 new pairs use one background template per task/mapping, yielding a deliberately narrow diagnostic. Background templates differ between mappings in four tasks, so this does not establish response-label invariance. All four predicted effects differ within each task/mapping and include both signs. Fresh histories expose a calibration limitation. The frozen controller's effects correlate with predicted effects (0.935 on familiar tasks; 0.838 on held-out tasks), but global recovery falls to 0.080 and $-0.426$. A mapping-synchronized within-task derangement changes every target and scores worse than the true target assignment ($p=1/1001$ for each task group, 1,000 draws). It does not rescue quantitative recovery or specificity against other subspaces: matched-random empirical $p$ values are 0.095 and 0.952, respectively, and some conventional baselines achieve higher recovery on the fresh diagnostic. \section{Broader fresh-context Qwen test}\label{app:freshcontexts}
A separate prospective protocol freezes all three retained controllers and their cognitive models. Four distinct current-state backgrounds per task are drawn with seed 87001. Four signed history patterns vary outcomes at lengths three and five while holding actions and current factors fixed. Context-isolated cue contrasts are added for bandit, foraging, and debugging. The resulting 640 rendered conditions represent 320 semantic states, with zero overlap with the original mechanistic manifest. Both response mappings share identical semantic backgrounds, and the standard matched-condition validator and loader pass. The explicit pair table preserves both positive and negative evaluation directions while the condition manifest uses increasing-target member ordering.

There are 416 computational pair rows: 224 ordinary-history pairs and 192 context-sensitive or held-out broad-history pairs. The two ordinary-history controllers each evaluate the 224-pair set; the context controller evaluates 192 pairs. Twenty new random subspaces match each controller's layer and rank and target its per-pair intervention norm; achieved norms differ slightly at bfloat16 precision. All controller and random outcomes are retained. Current-state backgrounds vary, but renderer wording remains fixed; this is not a broad linguistic-template test.

A secondary natural-effect diagnostic was added during the intervention run, with no fitting or selection. It evaluates the 320 unique natural source conditions and reuses the main run's cached base logits. After observing target miscalibration, intervention recovery against natural effects was specified for every method as a diagnostic endpoint. It is not an original preregistered endpoint and does not replace cognitive-target recovery. Background-level bootstrap resampling retains all histories and both response mappings together within each sampled context. Only four backgrounds per task and twenty random subspaces are available, so uncertainty and random-comparison resolution remain limited.

\section{Remaining validation}
The optimization follow-up covers one fixed primary controller, five seeds, and conventional/direct/random comparisons. The separate fresh-context test extends evaluation to all retained controllers, multiple backgrounds, and two history lengths. Broader confirmatory specificity still requires linguistic-template variation, more varied contexts, stronger cognitive-target calibration, and an adequately resolved random null. New designs must be frozen before additional neural evaluation and should not silently replace this failed calibration test.

The same-data baseline comparison fixes layer and rank. A claim about the efficiency or reliability of cognitive-guided \emph{discovery} additionally requires repeated layer/rank selection under declared compute budgets, as well as rank-1 comparisons that avoid a completion rule. Optimization stability and full-search stability are different quantities. The present results do not establish the latter.

Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct access and execution are now verified, but mechanistic replication remains incomplete. A feasibility pilot tested 490 semantic situations across seven tasks with both X/Y mappings (980 evaluations). All tasks failed the inherited counterbalance gate: mean absolute changes in continuation probability under label swapping ranged from 0.291 to 0.522, exceeding 0.25. A subsequently specified A/B correction and matched X/Y control used 490 fresh semantic situations (1,960 evaluations). A/B passed only bandit; X/Y again failed every task. All evaluations produced an action label as the top token. Identity patching on the real model had zero measured error and finite, nonzero alignment gradients, but a random rank-2 edit had zero measured forward effect at bfloat16 precision. These are feasibility diagnostics, not evidence of a replicated controller. Label identity and answer-option order require separate calibration and untouched validation before Llama behavioral models and causal targets can be fitted. The complete protocols and all outcomes are retained in the supplementary artifact package. A subsequent label-pair by option-order calibration used 140 new semantic situations and four formats (1,120 additional evaluations). Reversing semantic option order raised mean continuation probability from 0.337 to 0.812 with A/B and from 0.412 to 0.852 with X/Y. No format passed every task gate, so the reserved independent validation set was not evaluated. Total Llama behavioral evaluations are 4,060; these failures motivate response-interface validation before a mechanistic replication. The original sequential-bandit repository also needs an independent artifact audit before its reported figures are promoted from attributed context to revalidated results.
